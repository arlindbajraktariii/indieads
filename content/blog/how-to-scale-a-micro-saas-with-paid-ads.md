There is a moment when a small product starts working: a few customers arrive every week, retention is not embarrassing, and someone suggests increasing the ad budget. That suggestion is either the beginning of a business or the fastest way to burn a year of savings.

Here is how to tell the difference.

## Do not scale what is not working

Scaling multiplies whatever exists. If the landing page converts 1% of cold traffic, more traffic produces more 1% days. If onboarding loses half of new users, more budget produces more churn.

Before increasing spend, confirm:

- Organic traffic converts at a rate you understand.
- New users activate without a call.
- A meaningful share of them come back in week two.
- Cost per signup is below the value of a customer.

If any of these is false, the next euro should go into the product or the funnel, not the ad platform.

## Find the channel that repeats

A channel is scalable when the same setup produces similar results three times in a row. One good week is a coincidence; three is a pattern.

For each test, write down:

- Placement and audience
- Offer and landing page
- Spend, clicks, signups
- Cost per signup

If the third run looks like the first, you have a channel. If the numbers swing wildly, you have noise and a smaller budget problem.

## Unit economics before budget

The simple version:

```
value of a customer = monthly revenue × average lifetime (months)
max CAC             = value × margin you are comfortable with
```

If a customer pays €20/month and stays eight months, the value is €160. Spending €60 to acquire one leaves room for churn, refunds and the cost of serving them. Spending €150 does not, even if the campaign “works” in the short term.

Write the number down and treat it as a hard ceiling. The most common scaling mistake is celebrating signups while ignoring payback.

## Iterate on the two highest-leverage pieces

When a channel works, most of the remaining gain comes from two places:

1. **The message.** Test one new hook at a time. Keep the winner as the control.
2. **The landing page.** Improve the first screen, the proof, and the call to action.

Do not add new channels to escape the work of improving these two. A better funnel makes every future channel cheaper.

## Pace the budget

Sudden jumps in spend produce sudden jumps in noise. Increase by 20–30% at a time, and hold for at least a week before judging.

Watch for:

- Cost per signup drifting up as spend increases.
- Frequency rising in a small placement.
- Conversion rate falling because the page is attracting a broader audience.

If cost per signup doubles when you double spend, the channel has a ceiling. That is useful information, not a failure.

## When to diversify

Add a second channel when the first one is:

- Profitable at a stable cost per signup.
- Producing more volume than you can easily handle.
- Dependent on a single placement you do not control.

Diversification is insurance, not growth. Two working channels are better than five half-tested ones.

## The boring operating rhythm

- **Weekly:** review spend, signups and cost per signup.
- **Biweekly:** one message test, one page improvement.
- **Monthly:** decide whether to increase, hold or pause.
- **Quarterly:** check whether the channel still fits the product.

Scaling a micro-SaaS is not a growth hack. It is a small system, run consistently, with the discipline to stop when the numbers stop making sense.
