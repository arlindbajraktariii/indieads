There is no shortage of places to promote a SaaS product. There is a shortage of honest comparisons. Every list is either “50 directories you must submit to” or a thinly disguised ad for one channel.

This is a map instead: where the traffic comes from, what it costs, and when it is worth your time.

## Communities (high intent, high effort)

Reddit, Hacker News, Discord servers, Slack groups and niche forums.

**Good for:** early feedback, first users, and learning how people describe the problem.
**Bad for:** scaling. Communities punish repetition and overt promotion.
**Rule of thumb:** contribute for two weeks before you share anything of your own. Then share something useful, not a launch link.

If you only have time for one channel at the start, pick the community where your users already complain about the problem.

## Directories and marketplaces (low effort, variable return)

There are hundreds of startup and tool directories. Most send a small burst of curiosity and little else.

**Good for:** backlinks, a trickle of discovery, and social proof.
**Bad for:** sustained acquisition.
**Rule of thumb:** submit to the ten your audience actually reads. Skip the ones that require a paid “fast track” before you have revenue.

## Newsletters and podcasts (borrowed trust)

Sponsorships in niche newsletters and podcast reads are often the highest-quality paid placement for indie products, because the audience is curated and the context is relevant.

**Good for:** credibility and qualified traffic.
**Bad for:** measuring precisely. Attribution is fuzzy.
**Rule of thumb:** ask for the open rate, the click rate and the audience size. If the publisher will not share them, walk away.

## SEO and content (slow, compounding)

One page per problem, written better than the current results.

**Good for:** intent-driven traffic that keeps arriving after you stop working.
**Bad for:** impatient founders. Expect months, not days.
**Rule of thumb:** start with the questions your users ask in onboarding. Those are the pages that convert.

Our guide to [SEO beyond Google for indie hackers](/blog/seo-for-indie-hackers-beyond-google) covers the technical baseline.

## Ad networks (fast feedback, needs a funnel)

Paid placement gets you data quickly — if the destination converts. It is a multiplier, not a fix.

**Good for:** testing a message against a broader audience, and adding predictable reach once the funnel works.
**Bad for:** pre-product-market-fit products with no activation.
**Rule of thumb:** do not spend on ads until you can convert ten users from manual outreach.

## Integrations and partnerships (underrated)

Where your product plugs into another tool, that tool’s marketplace is a distribution channel. A listing in an integration directory reaches people at the moment they are connecting workflows.

**Good for:** high-intent users and long-tail traffic.
**Bad for:** products with no natural integration partner.
**Rule of thumb:** pick the platform your users already pay for. Build the integration properly, then ask for the listing.

## Product Hunt and launch platforms (a spike, not a strategy)

Launch day produces a bump, some feedback and a backlink. It rarely produces a business.

**Good for:** concentrated attention and social proof.
**Bad for:** retention. Most launch traffic never returns.
**Rule of thumb:** treat the launch as a campaign with a 48-hour window and a two-week follow-up, not as the plan.

## How to choose

Rank channels by two things: how close the audience is to the problem, and how quickly you can get a real signal.

| Channel | Signal speed | Effort | Compounds? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Communities | Days | High | Some |
| Newsletters | Weeks | Medium | No |
| SEO | Months | High | Yes |
| Paid ads | Days | Medium | No |
| Integrations | Months | High | Yes |
| Launch platforms | One week | Medium | No |

Start with one high-intent channel and one compounding channel. Add a third only when the first two are boring.
