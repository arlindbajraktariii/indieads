Most advice about advertising assumes you have a team. Someone writes the copy, someone designs the creative, someone else checks the numbers on Friday. When you are the whole company, that advice collapses into a to-do list you will never finish.

Here is a smaller version. It fits in a few evenings and it produces a real answer instead of a folder of half-finished experiments.

## Start with one channel, not five

The temptation is to “cover your bases”: a bit of social, a bit of search, a newsletter sponsorship, maybe a directory. Spreading a small budget across five channels guarantees that none of them gets enough data to mean anything.

Pick one channel where your audience already spends time. Run it for two to four weeks. Only then decide whether to continue, change the offer, or try somewhere else.

If you are not sure where to start, [this list of places to promote a SaaS](/blog/best-places-to-promote-your-saas-in-2026) is organized by effort and intent.

## Write the offer before the ad

An ad cannot fix a vague offer. Before you touch a campaign builder, answer these three questions in one sentence each:

- Who is this for?
- What changes for them after they sign up?
- What do they have to do next?

“A free trial” is not an offer. “Connect your repo and get a security report in ten minutes” is.

## Make a landing page that answers one question

The page should answer the question the ad created. If the ad says “track your ad spend without a spreadsheet”, the page should show ad spend tracking, not a tour of every feature.

Keep it short: headline, three proof points, one call to action. Remove the navigation if you can. Every extra link is an exit.

## Set a budget you can lose

Decide the amount before you launch, and treat it as tuition. If losing the money would change your month, the budget is too high.

For a first test, the goal is not profit. It is learning:

- Does the audience click?
- Does the page convert?
- Is the cost per signup in the same universe as the value of a customer?

If the answer to the last question is “no, and not close”, you learned something valuable for a small price.

## Measure one number

Pick one primary metric before the campaign starts. Cost per signup is usually the right one for an early product. Everything else — impressions, CTR, time on page — is supporting context.

Two numbers are allowed as secondary diagnostics:

1. **Click-through rate** tells you whether the message matches the audience.
2. **Landing page conversion rate** tells you whether the promise holds up.

If CTR is fine and conversion is terrible, the problem is the page or the offer. If CTR is terrible, the problem is the message or the placement. That is usually enough to know what to fix next.

## Do the boring follow-up

Most visitors will not buy on the first visit. If you have nothing to capture their email, you are paying for a one-night stand. A simple waitlist or newsletter is enough — and it compounds while you sleep.

## When it does not work

A failed first campaign is normal. Before you abandon the channel, check these in order:

- **Audience fit** — were these people plausibly in the market?
- **Message** — did the ad describe a real outcome?
- **Landing page** — did it keep the promise in the first screen?
- **Price** — did the offer ask for too much too early?

Change one variable at a time. If you change three, you will not know which one moved the number.

## A realistic first month

Week 1: write the offer, build the landing page, add tracking.
Week 2: launch one small campaign, check it daily for the first three days.
Week 3: leave it alone and collect data.
Week 4: review, write down what you learned, decide the next test.

That is it. No growth hacks, no ten-channel matrix. Just a loop small enough to finish and honest enough to learn from.
