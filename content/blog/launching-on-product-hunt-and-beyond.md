Launch day is the most scheduled and least understood moment in an indie product’s life. Founders spend weeks on the graphic and minutes on what happens after the traffic arrives.

Here is a realistic version of the sequence, based on how launch platforms actually behave — no guarantees, no growth hacks.

## What launch day does and does not do

A launch gives you a concentrated burst of attention and a backlink. It does not give you retention, and it rarely gives you a business.

The useful outcomes are:

- A few hundred people see the product who would never have found it otherwise.
- Feedback you could not get from friends.
- A handful of users who stick around.
- Proof for future visitors that the product exists.

The useless outcome is a ranking screenshot with no follow-up.

## Prepare the assets that matter

Most launch prep is cosmetic. These are the pieces that change the result:

1. **A landing page that works on mobile.** Most launch traffic is a phone in a queue.
2. **A one-sentence explanation.** If a visitor cannot repeat it, the headline is wrong.
3. **A demo.** A short video or an interactive sample converts better than a feature list.
4. **A reason to act today.** A waitlist, a free trial, a founding-user price. Something.
5. **A follow-up email.** The list you collect on launch day is the real asset.

Do not ship a new pricing model on launch day. Do not rewrite the product. Freeze the scope a week before.

## The 48-hour window

The rhythm of most launch platforms is short. Plan for two days of active presence:

- Reply to every comment with a real answer.
- Share the launch with communities where you are already a member — not as a drive-by.
- Watch for confusion: if three people ask the same question, fix the page that day.
- Do not refresh the ranking every five minutes. It does not change the outcome.

If the launch goes well, resist the urge to declare victory. If it goes badly, resist the urge to rewrite the product that night.

## The follow-up most founders skip

The spike ends. The useful work starts.

- **Email everyone who signed up** within 48 hours. One paragraph: what the product does, what to try first, how to reply.
- **Ask the new users one question**: “What were you hoping this would do for you?”
- **Fix the top onboarding problem** before the next campaign.
- **Write a short retro** with numbers and lessons. Future-you will need it.

Most launch traffic will not return. The ones who do are the signal.

## Beyond the launch platform

A launch is one campaign, not a channel. After it:

- **Publish the story.** A post about what you built and what you learned keeps working after the spike.
- **Submit to directories** that your audience actually reads. Ten good ones, not a hundred.
- **Pitch a newsletter** that covers your niche, with a specific angle and a clear offer.
- **Test a small paid campaign** if the funnel converted organically. Beware of spending on a page that has never converted.

## Measure the tail, not the spike

Track these for two weeks after launch:

- Signups from launch traffic.
- Activation — did they do the core action?
- Return visits in week two.
- Cost per signup if you paid for any promotion.

A launch that produces 500 visitors and 3 retained users is a success if those 3 teach you something. A launch that produces 5,000 visitors and zero retention is a content event, not a customer event.

## The unglamorous truth

Launch platforms reward preparation and timing, both of which you partly control. They do not reward neediness, and they cannot compensate for a product nobody needs.

Treat the launch as a rehearsal for the marketing you will do every month after it. The habit is the asset.
