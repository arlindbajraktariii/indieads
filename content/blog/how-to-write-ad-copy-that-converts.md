Most ad copy fails for a boring reason: it describes the product instead of the change. “AI-powered workflow automation” is a description. “Stop copying tickets into three tools” is a change someone can feel.

You do not need to be a copywriter. You need to be specific, brief and honest.

## Say what it is in six words

If a stranger cannot tell what you sell from the first six words, rewrite them.

- “Invoicing for freelancers who hate invoicing.”
- “A changelog your customers actually read.”
- “Find broken links before your users do.”

Six words force a decision about who the product is for. That decision is the ad.

## Lead with the outcome, not the feature

Features are evidence. Outcomes are reasons to click.

| Feature | Outcome |
| --- | --- |
| Real-time sync | Your data is never stale |
| Custom domains | Look like a real company |
| One-click exports | Get out of the tool when you need to |

Use features as proof after the promise, not as the promise itself.

## Specific beats clever

Clever copy makes people admire you. Specific copy makes them act.

- “Save time” is not specific.
- “Cut your weekly reporting from two hours to fifteen minutes” is.

Specificity also signals honesty. If you claim a number, be ready to explain where it comes from, or soften it to something true.

## Match the ad to the landing page

The most common conversion leak is a mismatch between the promise and the page. If the ad says “ship a changelog in five minutes”, the page should not open with your company story.

One rule: the headline of the landing page should be a direct continuation of the ad. Same words, same promise, same audience. Anything else is a tax on attention.

## Three formulas that work at small scale

**1. Problem → consequence → fix**

> Still tracking ad spend in a spreadsheet?
> One missed row and the monthly report is wrong.
> RoketAd shows spend, clicks and signups in one place.

**2. Before → after**

> Before: five tabs to publish one changelog entry.
> After: one editor, one click, your domain.

**3. Proof → offer**

> Used by 400 developers to catch broken links weekly.
> Try it free on your repo.

The formulas are scaffolding, not rules. The words inside them still have to be true.

## Write for one person

The best-performing ads read like a message to a specific person, not a broadcast to a demographic. Name the role, the tool, the moment.

- “For founders who just shipped and have no idea where to post.”
- “For developers maintaining a docs site with dead links.”

When someone recognizes themselves in the first line, the rest of the ad has permission to exist.

## What to test first

Do not test ten things. Test in this order:

1. **The hook** — does the first line stop the right person?
2. **The offer** — is the next step clear and low-friction?
3. **The destination** — does the page keep the promise?

Creative style, colors and emoji come later. A beautiful ad with a vague promise loses to a plain ad with a sharp one.

## The one test before you spend

Show the ad to five people in your audience. Ask: “What do you think this product does, and who is it for?”

If three of five get it right, run it. If not, the copy is not ready — and no amount of budget will fix that.
